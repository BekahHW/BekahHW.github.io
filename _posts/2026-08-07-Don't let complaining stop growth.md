Complaining has a place in life and in community. We don't want to fall into the trap of performing our best selves all the time. We want to be able to say, "This is hard," and have someone hear us.

People complain when something has hurt, disappointed, exhausted, or made them feel powerless. The complaint is usually pointing at something real.

A job search is a good example. You can do everything you were told to do. You can tailor your resume for every job, apply to 10+ jobs a day, customize your cover letters, send thank you messages, ask for feedback, show up to events, and even sound confident when nothing could be further from the truth.

And then silence. It's the kind of silence that wears a person down.

And if you don't have an outlet for that complaint, the silence can start to be consuming until it feels soul crushing. I've been there. There is value in being heard and validated.

So no, I don’t think the answer is to tell people to stop complaining. That usually makes people feel more alone, and loneliness is already part of the problem.

But I do think we have to be honest about the line between being witnessed and staying stuck.

## Complaining can build connection, and that’s why it works

Over the past handful of years, I've found it really useful to understand *why* patterns in behavior happen, like dive into the Huberman version of what's going on. There is a reason complaining feels useful.

When someone says, “This is awful,” and another person says, “Yes, it really is,” the first person feels less alone. The frustration has somewhere to go. The emotion gets named out loud instead of sitting unspoken in the body.

That part matters.

[Researchers who study rumination have found that repeatedly talking through problems can be associated with stronger friendships, but it can also be associated with more anxiety and depressive symptoms.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3382075/)

That tracks. The same conversation can make you feel known and keep you anchored to the same wound. That’s the part we need to watch.

The goal of community cannot ever be to make discouragement more comfortable forever. A good community gives you room to tell the truth, then helps you find the next truthful step.

## Stuckness can become a brain pattern

I’m not a neuroscientist, but I am someone who cares about what happens to people when disappointment turns into identity.

The neuroscience is useful because it gives us a more compassionate way to understand stuckness.

Learned helplessness research has changed over time. The older framing was that people or animals “learned” nothing they did mattered. More recent neuroscience complicates that. Maier and Seligman argue that [passivity after uncontrollable stress may be closer to a default response, and that what has to be learned is control. That learning involves prefrontal systems detecting agency and helping regulate stress responses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27337390/).

If someone has tried over and over and still hasn’t gotten traction, their brain may start protecting them from the cost of trying again. It may become easier to complain than to risk one more failed attempt.

That doesn't make the person lazy. It also doesn't mean the person is finished. The work is to rebuild evidence of control in small enough pieces that the brain can tolerate trying again.

## The job search breaks momentum before it breaks effort

The job search is awful right now. And I'm so sorry to see so many people struggle through it. I hear stories over and over and over that validate that effort and outcome are painfully disconnected.

You can send twenty applications and hear nothing, have a great conversation and still get passed over, improve your portfolio and have no idea whether anyone noticed, and be qualified and still lose to someone with a referral, a better market fit, or better timing. 

That makes the job search a perfect place for helplessness to grow. The person may still be trying, but the trying stops feeling connected to anything. Then the complaint becomes the only part of the process that gives immediate relief.

The application gives silence.
The complaint gives validation.

It's natural for the brain to reach for the thing that gives something back, but validation isn't the same as momentum. If all you have is validation, you might feel understood while your actual situation remains unchanged.

## The first move is to separate the feeling from the conclusion

When someone says, “I’m never going to find a job,” I don’t think the best response is, “That’s not true.”

It might be true that they are exhausted.
It might be true that they feel rejected.
It might be true that the market is brutal.
It might be true that their current strategy is not working.
It might be true that they need help.

The sentence “I’m never going to find a job” is doing too much work.

So we need to encourage starting by separating the feeling from the conclusion.

I know this is hard, but it is possible to name the feeling without letting it define the outcome.

“I feel embarrassed that I’m still looking.”

“I feel angry that I keep doing the work and getting no response.”

“I feel scared that nothing I try is changing the outcome.”

Those are cleaner sentences, and I'm sure they're painful, but they are workable. Because once the real feeling is named, the next question changes.

You're no longer asking, “What is wrong with me?”

You can ask, “What part of this process needs a different input?”

That question isn’t small. It's the first step toward agency. 

## A complaint needs a conversion point

I don’t think we should ban complaining from communities. That would make people less honest, but I do think every healthy community needs a conversion point.

A conversion point is the moment the conversation moves from pain to agency. It can be gentle:

“You’re right. That sounds exhausting. Do you want help thinking through the next step?”

“I get why you’re frustrated. Let's look at what happened in the last five applications.”

When I lost a job early in my tech career, a friend said to me, "Ok, so self-evaluation time. What's next? What can you do differently?" That was a really hard question to hear at the time, but it immediately changed my perspective and let me move to the next thing.  

## Small actions teach control again

When someone is discouraged, big advice can make things worse.

- Build a new portfolio.
- Rebrand yourself.
- Apply to fifty jobs.
- Network every day.
- Learn a new framework.
- Start posting consistently.

Maybe some of that is useful later. But for a person who is already overwhelmed, it can sound like one more way to fail. So sometimes we ahve to start smaller.

Implementation intention research gives us a practical structure for this. People are more likely to follow through when they make a specific plan for when, where, and how they will act. The common format is: if this happens, then I will do that. 

If I get a rejection, then I will wait ten minutes before deciding what it means about me.

If it is Monday at 9 AM, then I will send one message to someone in my network.

If I catch myself doom-scrolling job posts, then I will save one role and write down the actual requirements.

If I feel too embarrassed to ask for help, then I will send the shortest honest message I can.

It can be easier if you start with one small action. That's how you start giving your brain different evidence.

## Track the actions that still count

When someone is discouraged, it can feel like nothing they do matters. That is a lie. The work still counts, even if the market doesn't respond.

[Job-search research has linked job-search self-efficacy, motivation, and persistence with the search process and employment outcomes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03377). That doesn't mean every person can effort their way through a broken market. It means people need ways to keep acting when the market gives them very little feedback.

A tracker can be a witness to remind you that you are still participating in your own life.

## Behavioral activation gives momentum a shape

There is another useful idea here: behavioral activation. Behavioral activation is used in mental health treatment, especially for depression. [The basic idea is that action can help interrupt avoidance and low mood by reconnecting people with meaningful or rewarding activities](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013305_behavioural-activation-therapy-depression-adults).

That's a reminder that waiting to feel motivated can keep you waiting for a very long time.

Sometimes the sequence has to be:

Move first.
Feel later.

Send the message before you feel brave. Open the document before you feel clear. Take the walk before you feel hopeful. Ask for help before you feel deserving of it.

The feeling may not change immediately. Do it anyway.

Not because your feelings are fake. Because your feelings are real, and they do not get to be the only force driving the day.

## Your public posture becomes part of the job search

If you are looking for work, people look at how you show up online. That doesn't mean you need to become fake. It doesn't mean every post needs to be polished into corporate language. It doesn't mean you should hide the fact that the job search is hard.

But your public presence is part of the *signal*. If every visible post is bitterness, blame, despair, or contempt, people notice.

We all agree that the market is hard. We understand why you are tired. Most of us want better systems for hiring, training, and entry-level work. *But* you don't want people reading your posts wondering what it would feel like to work with you.

That's the hard part.

A hiring manager is trying to answer more than one question. Can this person do the work? Can this person recover from frustration? Can this person take feedback? Can this person stay steady when the answer is unclear? Can this person be honest without making everyone else carry the full weight of their discouragement?

Those questions may feel unfair when you are already exhausted. They are still being asked, so tell the truth purposefully:

“This job search has been brutal, and I’m discouraged” is honest.

“This has been a hard week, so I’m tightening my resume and asking two people for feedback” is honest with movement attached.

“I got rejected again, and I’m taking the weekend to reset before I send the next batch” is honest with a recovery plan.

That's different from posting the same helpless complaint every day with no visible attempt to change the input.

One makes people want to help, and the other makes people step back.

## Resilience has to become visible

I don’t want communities where people are afraid to admit they are struggling. I want communities where people can tell the truth and still be invited back into agency.

But at some point, adults have to get back up.

That sentence has an edge to it, but it's because I think it needs one.

Because there is a kind of support that accidentally trains fragility. Validating feelings is important, but not when it prevents forward motion and treats it like an unwanted demand. That's not care. Care tells the truth and stays in the room.

The truth is that rejection hurts. The truth is that the market can be brutal.The truth is that some people have more access, more money, more connections, and more margin. The truth is that discouragement can change your brain and your body. The truth is that you may need help. The truth is that your resume and skills might need work. Your communication might need to be clearer. Your portfolio might need to be more visible. Your network might need to be bigger. Your timing might need to be better.

And the truth is that you still have to move.

It doesn't all have to be dramatic. We can start one rep at a time. Read your resume, send a follow-up message, ask questions, track the things you do, practice your answers, take a walk outside, plan things that feel manageable, but above all, don't stay stuck. 

If you've been complaining about the same problem for more than three conversations, stop and write down what action came after each one.

If the answer is “nothing,” there's your data. It's not a moral failing. It's not to shame yourself. It's to identify a pattern that can be changed.

A complaint can tell you where something hurts. It can show you what feels unfair, what feels blocked, what feels too heavy to carry alone. But the complaint cannot become the whole practice.

You still have to train. You still have to build evidence that you can act when the outcome is uncertain.You still have to become the kind of person who can take a hard week, tell the truth about it, and make the next right move anyway.

Start small if you need to.

But start.
